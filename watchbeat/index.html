WatchBeat listens to a mechanical watch through your iPhone's microphone and tells you how it's running: how many seconds a day it gains or loses, its beat error, and its balance amplitude. All of the analysis happens on your iPhone.

What you need

  • A ticking mechanical watch (hand-wound or automatic). Quartz (battery) watches and smartwatches can't be measured; WatchBeat will tell you if it hears a quartz watch.
  • An iPhone running iOS 26 or later.
  • A reasonably quiet room.

Taking a reading

  1. Press the bottom edge of your iPhone, where the microphone is, against the watch crystal. The watch can stay on your wrist.
  2. Tap Listen and hold steady. The screen shows the sound it's picking up while it works. Most readings finish in about 15 seconds; a quiet watch can take up to a minute.
  3. Read your result.

If the signal is weak, try pressing the phone against the caseback instead of the crystal.

Understanding the result

Rate
How many seconds per day the watch gains (+) or loses (−). WatchBeat also shows the beat rate it identified, in beats per hour (bph) and Hz.
Beat error
The difference in timing between the "tick" and the "tock", in milliseconds. Under 1 ms is shown as good, 1–3 ms as fair, and over 3 ms as high.
Amplitude
How far the balance wheel swings, in degrees. The calculation depends on your movement's lift angle: enter it in the Lift Angle field (the default is 50°; check your movement's specifications). Amplitude shows "---" when it can't be measured reliably.
Timegraph
Each dot is one tick (blue) or tock (cyan) from the analyzed recording. A tight, straight trace means steady ticking; two separate lines indicate beat error.
Measurement quality
How cleanly the ticks came through. A low score means the reading is less certain.

Position Study

A watch runs a little differently depending on how it's held, so watchmakers check it in several positions. Tap Position Study for a guided measurement in the five standard positions: Dial Up, Dial Down, 6 Up, 3 Up and 9 Up.

Hold the watch crystal against the phone's bottom edge with the crown pointing right, and keep that grip throughout. The app shows how to hold the phone for each position and starts each reading by itself once you're holding it steady. The summary shows the average rate, the spread between positions, and each position's rate, beat error and amplitude.

When a reading doesn't work

Couldn't find a clear signal

  • Move to a quieter room.
  • Hold the phone firmly against the watch.
  • Try the caseback instead of the crystal.
  • Remove a thick phone case.

Reading wasn't conclusive

  • Try again; a fresh recording often reads cleanly.
  • If it keeps happening, try a different watch position.

Unexpected rate

  • If you know your watch's beat rate, check whether the measured value matches.
  • Try a different watch position, and press the phone firmly against the watch.

Too far off to measure

The reading came out more than about ±2,000 seconds (half an hour) a day off, which is beyond what WatchBeat can read reliably. Most often, background noise such as music, voices or running water was mistaken for ticking: try again somewhere quiet. If a quiet room gives a similar result, the watch is probably far out of adjustment and worth taking to a watchmaker.

Quartz detected

WatchBeat measures mechanical watches only.

Microphone unavailable

  • End any phone or video call.
  • Quit other apps that are recording (Voice Memos, for example).
  • Check that WatchBeat is allowed in Settings → Privacy & Security → Microphone.

Sending a debug report

If a reading looks wrong, tap Send Debug on the result or error screen. The report contains the recording from that measurement (which can pick up other sounds in the room), the measurement details, and your app and iOS versions. You can add the watch's name and a note. Nothing goes out until you send it yourself from Mail or the share sheet. See the privacy policy.
